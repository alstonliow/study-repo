A mixture of Gaussians is GDA (@def-gda) with the labels hidden: each point comes from one of $k$ Gaussian clusters, but we are not told which.

::: {#def-gaussian-mixture .definition name="Mixture of Gaussians"}

A *mixture of $k$ Gaussians* on $\mathbb{R}^d$ has parameters $\phi_1,\dots,\phi_k\ge0$ with $\sum_j\phi_j=1$, means $\boldsymbol\mu_j$ and positive definite covariances $\Sigma_j$. A point is generated by drawing a *latent* (hidden) label $z\in\{1,\dots,k\}$ with $P(z=j)=\phi_j$ and then $\mathbf{x}\mid z=j\sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$. The density of $\mathbf{x}$ is

$$
p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^k\phi_j\,\mathcal N(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j),
$$

where $\mathcal N(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ is the Gaussian density of @thm-jointly-gaussian-properties.

:::

The log-likelihood $\sum_i\log\sum_j\phi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$ has a sum inside the logarithm, and setting its gradient to zero gives no closed form. If the labels $z^{(i)}$ were known, the estimates would be those of GDA: class frequencies, class means and class covariances. EM alternates between guessing the labels softly and refitting.

::: {#def-em-gmm .definition name="EM for a mixture of Gaussians" related="def-expectation-maximization"}

Starting from initial parameters, repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving:

1. *E-step*: for every point $i$ and component $j$, compute the *responsibility*

   $$
   w_j^{(i)}=P\bigl(z^{(i)}=j\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\bigr)=\frac{\phi_j\,\mathcal N(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^k\phi_l\,\mathcal N(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\mu_l,\Sigma_l)}.
   $$

2. *M-step*: with $N_j=\sum_iw_j^{(i)}$, set

   $$
   \phi_j=\frac{N_j}N,\qquad \boldsymbol\mu_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_iw_j^{(i)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\qquad \Sigma_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_iw_j^{(i)}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\mu_j\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\mu_j\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.
   $$

:::

```{=latex}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{EM for a mixture of Gaussians}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT Points $\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}$, number of components $k$, initial $\phi,\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma$
\REPEAT
    \FOR{$i=1,\dots,N$ and $j=1,\dots,k$}
        \STATE $w_j^{(i)}\gets\phi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)\big/\sum_l\phi_l\mathcal N(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\mu_l,\Sigma_l)$ \COMMENT{E-step}
    \ENDFOR
    \FOR{$j=1,\dots,k$}
        \STATE $N_j\gets\sum_iw_j^{(i)}$, $\phi_j\gets N_j/N$, $\boldsymbol\mu_j\gets\sum_iw_j^{(i)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}/N_j$ \COMMENT{M-step}
        \STATE $\Sigma_j\gets\sum_iw_j^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\mu_j)(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\mu_j)^{\mathsf T}/N_j$
    \ENDFOR
\UNTIL{the log-likelihood changes by less than a tolerance}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

::: {#exm-em-gmm-step .example name="One EM iteration on four points"}

Fit $k=2$ components to the points $0,1,4,5$ on the line, starting from $\phi=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $\mu=(0,1)$ and variances $(1,1)$.

*E-step.* The responsibilities of component $1$ are $0.6225,\ 0.3775,\ 0.0293,\ 0.0110$ for the four points (those of component $2$ are one minus these). For example, at $x=0$ the ratio of the two densities is $e^{-0}/e^{-1/2}$, so $w_1=1/(1+e^{-1/2})\approx0.6225$.

*M-step.* $N_1\approx1.0403$ and $N_2\approx2.9597$, so

$$
\phi\approx(0.260,\ 0.740),\qquad \mu\approx(0.528,\ 3.193),\qquad \sigma^2\approx(0.799,\ 3.617).
$$

Component $2$ has moved to cover the two far points and widened to keep the point $1$; further iterations separate the two groups $\{0,1\}$ and $\{4,5\}$.

::: {#rmk-gaussian-mixtures-1 .remark name="Soft k-means"}

The E-step is a soft version of the assignment step of $k$-means and the M-step a weighted version of its update step. If every covariance is fixed at $\sigma^2I$, the weights are equal and $\sigma\to0$, each responsibility tends to $1$ for the nearest mean and $0$ for the others, and EM becomes $k$-means (@def-k-means). Like $k$-means, EM finds only a local optimum, and a component that collapses onto a single point drives the likelihood to $+\infty$; in practice one restarts, or adds a small multiple of $I$ to each $\Sigma_j$.

:::
