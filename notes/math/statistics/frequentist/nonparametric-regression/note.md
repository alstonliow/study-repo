A linear model assumes that the regression function $f(x)=E[Y\mid X=x]$ is linear in $x$, or in a few chosen features of $x$. Without such an assumption one can still estimate $f$ if it is smooth: near any point $x$, $f$ is nearly constant, so the responses of observations with $X_i$ near $x$ are approximately $f(x)$ plus noise, and their average estimates $f(x)$.

::: {#def-local-average-estimator .definition name="Local average estimator"}

Let $(X_i,Y_i)$, $i=1,\dots,n$, satisfy $Y_i=f(X_i)+\varepsilon_i$ with $X_i\in\mathbb{R}$, independent noise with $E[\varepsilon_i]=0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2$. For a *bandwidth* (window size) $h>0$ and a point $x$, let $I_x=\{i:|X_i-x|<h\}$. The *local average estimator* is

$$
\hat f_{n,h}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1{|I_x|}\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_x}Y_i, & I_x\ne\emptyset,\\[2ex] 0, & I_x=\emptyset.\end{cases}
$$

:::

```{=latex}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Local average estimate of a regression function on a grid}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT data $(X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)$; bandwidth $h>0$; evaluation points $x_1,\dots,x_m$
\FOR{$k=1,\dots,m$}
    \STATE $I\gets\{i:|X_i-x_k|<h\}$
    \IF{$I\ne\emptyset$}
        \STATE $\hat f(x_k)\gets\frac1{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}Y_i$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\hat f(x_k)\gets0$ \COMMENT{no data in the window}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $\hat f(x_1),\dots,\hat f(x_m)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

::: {#prp-local-average-bias-variance .proposition name="Bias and variance of a local average"}

Treat the design $X_1,\dots,X_n$ as fixed and suppose $I_x\ne\emptyset$. Then

$$
E\bigl[\hat f_{n,h}(x)\bigr]-f(x)=\frac1{|I_x|}\sum_{i\in I_x}\bigl(f(X_i)-f(x)\bigr),\qquad \mathrm{Var}\bigl(\hat f_{n,h}(x)\bigr)=\frac{\sigma^2}{|I_x|}.
$$

If moreover $|f(u)-f(v)|\le L|u-v|$ for all $u,v$, the bias is at most $Lh$ in absolute value, and the quadratic risk satisfies
$$E\bigl[(\hat f_{n,h}(x)-f(x))^2\bigr]\le L^2h^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{|I_x|}.$$

:::

::: {.proof}

1. $\hat f_{n,h}(x)=\frac1{|I_x|}\sum_{i\in I_x}\bigl(f(X_i)+\varepsilon_i\bigr)$. Its expectation is $\frac1{|I_x|}\sum_{i\in I_x}f(X_i)$, which gives the bias formula after subtracting $f(x)=\frac1{|I_x|}\sum_{i\in I_x}f(x)$.

2. The noise terms are independent with variance $\sigma^2$, so the variance of their average over $|I_x|$ indices is $\sigma^2/|I_x|$.

3. Each $i\in I_x$ has $|X_i-x|<h$, so $|f(X_i)-f(x)|\le Lh$ and the average of these terms is at most $Lh$.

4. The risk bound follows from quadratic risk $=$ bias$^2+$ variance (@prp-mse-bias-variance).

:::

::: {#rmk-bandwidth-choice .remark name="Choosing the bandwidth"}

The bandwidth trades bias against variance. As $h\to0$ each window contains a single observation and $\hat f$ reproduces the noisy data (*overfitting*: no bias, variance $\sigma^2$). As $h\to\infty$ every window contains all the data and $\hat f\equiv\bar Y_n$ (*underfitting*: tiny variance, large bias). With roughly $nh$ points per window the risk bound is of order $L^2h^2+\sigma^2/(nh)$, minimised by $h$ of order $n^{-1/3}$; if the smoothness $L$ is unknown, $h$ is chosen from the data, for example by cross validation (@def-cross-validation).

::: {#rmk-density-estimation .remark name="The same idea for densities"}

A density $f$ can be estimated the same way: the fraction of observations within $h$ of $x$, divided by the window length $2h$,
$$\hat f(x)=\frac1{2nh}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf 1\{|X_i-x|\le h\},$$
estimates $\Pr(|X-x|\le h)/(2h)\approx f(x)$. Again a small $h$ gives high variance and a large $h$ high bias.

:::
