The exponential response formula fails when the input frequency is a characteristic root. Then the response grows like a power of $t$: this is *pure resonance*. Close to resonance, the response shows *beats*.

::: {#thm-generalized-erf .theorem name="Resonant response formula"}
If $r$ is a root of $p$ of multiplicity exactly $m$ (so $p(r)=\dots=p^{(m-1)}(r)=0\neq p^{(m)}(r)$), then
$$
p(D)\,y=e^{rt}\quad\text{has the solution}\quad y_p=\frac{t^m\,e^{rt}}{p^{(m)}(r)} .
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. Write $p(s)=(s-r)^m\,g(s)$ with $g(r)\neq0$. Differentiating $m$ times and setting $s=r$ gives $p^{(m)}(r)=m!\,g(r)$.

2. By the exponential shift rule (@prp-operator-rules), $p(D)(e^{rt}t^m)=e^{rt}p(D+r)t^m=e^{rt}g(D+r)\,D^mt^m=e^{rt}g(D+r)\,m!$.

3. On a constant, $g(D+r)$ acts as multiplication by $g(r)$, so $p(D)(t^me^{rt})=m!\,g(r)\,e^{rt}=p^{(m)}(r)\,e^{rt}$.

4. Dividing by $p^{(m)}(r)$ gives the formula.
:::

::: {#exm-pure-resonance .example name="Pure resonance"}
An undamped spring driven at its natural frequency: $y''+\omega_0^2y=\cos\omega_0t$. Here $p(s)=s^2+\omega_0^2$, $p(i\omega_0)=0$, $p'(i\omega_0)=2i\omega_0$, so
$$
y_p=\operatorname{Re}\frac{t\,e^{i\omega_0t}}{2i\omega_0}=\frac{t\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0},
$$
an oscillation whose amplitude grows linearly without bound. Likewise $y''-y'-2y=e^{2t}$ has $p(2)=0$, $p'(2)=3$ and the solution $\tfrac13te^{2t}$.
:::

Pure resonance for $y''+y=\cos t$: the response $\tfrac12t\sin t$ grows linearly inside the envelope $\pm t/2$.

::: {#exm-beats .example name="Beats"}
For $\omega\neq\omega_0$, the solution of $y''+\omega_0^2y=\cos\omega t$ with $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ is
$$
y=\frac{\cos\omega t-\cos\omega_0t}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}=\frac{2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0-\omega)t}{2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0+\omega)t}{2}.
$$

1. The first form is the particular solution $\cos\omega t/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$ from @thm-sinusoidal-response plus the homogeneous solution that fixes the initial conditions.
2. The second form uses $\cos A-\cos B=2\sin\tfrac{B-A}{2}\sin\tfrac{B+A}{2}$.

When $\omega$ is close to $\omega_0$, a fast oscillation at about $\omega_0$ is modulated by a slow envelope of frequency $|\omega_0-\omega|/2$: the amplitude swells and fades, as heard when two nearly equal notes are played together.
