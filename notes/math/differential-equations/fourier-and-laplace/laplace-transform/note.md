The Laplace transform turns differentiation into multiplication by $s$, so a linear differential equation with given initial values becomes an algebraic equation.

::: {#def-laplace-transform .definition name="Laplace transform"}
For $f$ defined on $t\ge0$, the *Laplace transform* is
$$
F(s)=\mathcal L(f)(s)=\int_{0^-}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt ,
$$
for those complex $s$ where the integral converges. If $|f(t)|\le Me^{at}$ for large $t$ ($f$ of *exponential type* $a$), it converges for $\operatorname{Re}s>a$. The lower limit $0^-$ lets $f$ contain $\delta(t)$.
:::

For $f=1$ the integrand $e^{-st}$ decays faster as $s$ grows, and the area under it is $\mathcal L(1)(s)=1/s$.

:::

::: {#prp-laplace-table .proposition name="Basic transforms"}
For $\operatorname{Re}s$ large enough,
$$
\mathcal L(1)=\frac1s,\quad \mathcal L(e^{at})=\frac1{s-a},\quad \mathcal L(\cos\omega t)=\frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2},\quad \mathcal L(\sin\omega t)=\frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2},
$$
$$
\mathcal L(t^n)=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad \mathcal L(\delta(t-a))=e^{-as}\ (a\ge0).
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. $\int_0^\infty e^{(a-s)t}dt=\frac{1}{s-a}$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>\operatorname{Re}a$; $a=0$ gives $\mathcal L(1)$.

2. With $a=\pm i\omega$ and $\cos\omega t=\frac12(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t})$, $\sin\omega t=\frac1{2i}(e^{i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega t})$, linearity gives the two trigonometric transforms.

3. Integrating by parts, $\mathcal L(t^n)=\frac ns\mathcal L(t^{n-1})$, and induction from $\mathcal L(1)=\frac1s$ gives $n!/s^{n+1}$.

4. The sifting property (@def-delta-function) gives $\int\delta(t-a)e^{-st}dt=e^{-as}$.
:::

::: {#prp-laplace-rules .proposition name="Rules of the Laplace transform"}
With $F=\mathcal L(f)$ and $a\ge0$:

1. *s-shift:* $\mathcal L\bigl(e^{at}f(t)\bigr)=F(s-a)$ (any complex $a$);
2. *t-shift:* $\mathcal L\bigl(u(t-a)f(t-a)\bigr)=e^{-as}F(s)$;
3. *t-derivative:* $\mathcal L(f')=sF(s)-f(0^-)$ and $\mathcal L(f'')=s^2F(s)-sf(0^-)-f'(0^-)$;
4. *s-derivative:* $\mathcal L\bigl(t\,f(t)\bigr)=-F'(s)$.
:::

::: {.proof}
1. $\int e^{at}f(t)e^{-st}dt=\int f(t)e^{-(s-a)t}dt=F(s-a)$.

2. Substituting $\tau=t-a$: $\int_a^\infty f(t-a)e^{-st}dt=e^{-as}\int_0^\infty f(\tau)e^{-s\tau}d\tau$.

3. Integrating by parts, $\int_{0^-}^\infty f'e^{-st}dt=\bigl[fe^{-st}\bigr]_{0^-}^\infty+s\int fe^{-st}dt=-f(0^-)+sF(s)$, the term at $\infty$ vanishing for $\operatorname{Re}s$ large; applying this to $f'$ gives the second formula.

4. Differentiating under the integral sign, $F'(s)=\int(-t)f(t)e^{-st}dt$.
:::

The t-shift rule: delaying a signal by $a$ (and switching it off before $a$) multiplies its transform by $e^{-as}$.

:::

::: {#thm-laplace-convolution-rule .theorem name="Convolution rule" related="thm-convolution-rule"}
$$
\mathcal L(f*g)=F(s)\,G(s).
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. $\mathcal L(f*g)=\int_0^\infty\int_0^tf(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau\,e^{-st}\,dt$, an integral over the region $0\le\tau\le t$.

2. Exchanging the order of integration: $\int_0^\infty f(\tau)\int_\tau^\infty g(t-\tau)e^{-st}\,dt\,d\tau$.

3. With $v=t-\tau$, the inner integral is $e^{-s\tau}\int_0^\infty g(v)e^{-sv}dv=e^{-s\tau}G(s)$.

4. So the whole is $G(s)\int_0^\infty f(\tau)e^{-s\tau}d\tau=F(s)G(s)$.
:::

::: {#exm-laplace-rules .example name="Using the rules"}
1. $\mathcal L(te^{-t})=-\frac{d}{ds}\frac1{s+1}=\frac{1}{(s+1)^2}$.
2. $\mathcal L(e^{-t}\sin2t)=\frac{2}{(s+1)^2+4}$ by the s-shift.
3. $\mathcal L(t*t)=\frac1{s^2}\cdot\frac1{s^2}=\frac{3!}{6\,s^4}$, consistent with $t*t=\tfrac{t^3}6$.
:::
