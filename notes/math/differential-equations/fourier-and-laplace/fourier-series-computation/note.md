A periodic input can be split into sinusoids of frequencies $\omega,2\omega,3\omega,\dots$. This note gives the coefficient formulas for any period and the shortcuts that avoid most integrals.

::: {#def-fourier-series-period .definition name="Fourier series of a periodic function" generalizes="thm-fourier-coefficients"}
Let $f$ be piecewise continuous with period $2L$, and $\omega=\pi/L$. Its *Fourier series* is
$$
f(t)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\bigl(a_n\cos n\omega t+b_n\sin n\omega t\bigr),
$$
$$
a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt,\qquad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(t)\sin n\omega t\,dt .
$$
The integrals may be taken over any interval of length $2L$. The term $n=1$ is the *fundamental*, the others are *harmonics*.
:::

::: {#thm-fourier-convergence .theorem name="Convergence of Fourier series"}
If $f$ has period $2L$ and is piecewise smooth (finitely many jumps per period, with $f$ and $f'$ piecewise continuous), its Fourier series converges at every $t$ to
$$
\tfrac12\bigl(f(t^-)+f(t^+)\bigr),
$$
which is $f(t)$ where $f$ is continuous and the midpoint of the jump where it is not.
:::

::: {#prp-even-odd-fourier .proposition name="Even and odd functions"}
If $f$ is even, then $b_n=0$ and $a_n=\dfrac2L\displaystyle\int_0^Lf(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt$ (a *cosine series*). If $f$ is odd, then $a_n=0$ and $b_n=\dfrac2L\displaystyle\int_0^Lf(t)\sin n\omega t\,dt$ (a *sine series*).
:::

::: {.proof}
1. The product of an even and an odd function is odd, and the integral of an odd function over $[-L,L]$ is $0$.

2. For even $f$, $f(t)\sin n\omega t$ is odd, so $b_n=0$; $f(t)\cos n\omega t$ is even, so its integral over $[-L,L]$ is twice that over $[0,L]$.

3. For odd $f$ the roles of cosine and sine are exchanged.
:::

::: {#exm-triangle-wave .example name="A triangle wave"}
Let $f(t)=|t|$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$, extended with period $2\pi$. It is even, so $b_n=0$; $a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi t\,dt=\pi$, and integrating by parts
$$
a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi t\cos nt\,dt=\frac{2\bigl((-1)^n-1\bigr)}{\pi n^2}.
$$
Hence $|t|=\dfrac\pi2-\dfrac4\pi\left(\cos t+\dfrac{\cos3t}{9}+\dfrac{\cos5t}{25}+\cdots\right)$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$. The coefficients decay like $1/n^2$ because $f$ is continuous.
:::

Let $f(t)=t$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$, extended with period $2\pi$. It is odd, and $b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi t\sin nt\,dt=\dfrac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$, so
$$
f(t)=2\left(\sin t-\frac{\sin2t}{2}+\frac{\sin3t}{3}-\cdots\right).
$$
At the jumps $t=\pm\pi$ the series gives $0$, the midpoint, as @thm-fourier-convergence predicts. The coefficients decay only like $1/n$ because $f$ jumps.

The sawtooth and its partial sum with 12 terms. At the jumps the series converges to the midpoint $0$ (dots); near them the partial sums overshoot (the Gibbs phenomenon).

:::

::: {#prp-fourier-operations .proposition name="Operations on Fourier series"}
1. *Linearity:* the series of $\alpha f+\beta g$ is $\alpha$ times that of $f$ plus $\beta$ times that of $g$.
2. *Shift and scale:* substituting $t\mapsto ct+d$ in the series of $f$ gives the series of $f(ct+d)$.
3. *Differentiation:* if $f$ is continuous, periodic and piecewise smooth, the series of $f'$ is obtained by differentiating the series of $f$ term by term.
:::

::: {.proof}
1. The coefficient formulas are linear in $f$.

2. Each term $\cos n\omega t$, $\sin n\omega t$ becomes a combination of $\cos n\omega(ct+d)$ and $\sin n\omega(ct+d)$, which is the series of $f(ct+d)$ with frequency $c\omega$; uniqueness of coefficients for that period identifies it.

3. Integrating by parts over one period, $\frac1L\int f'(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt=\frac1L\bigl[f\cos n\omega t\bigr]+\frac{n\omega}L\int f\sin n\omega t\,dt=n\omega\,b_n$, because the boundary term vanishes for continuous periodic $f$; similarly the sine coefficient of $f'$ is $-n\omega\,a_n$. These are the coefficients of the differentiated series.
:::

::: {#rmk-fourier-series-computation-1 .remark name="Differentiating the triangle wave"}
The triangle wave of @exm-triangle-wave is continuous, so differentiating its series gives the series of its derivative, the square wave equal to $-1$ on $(-\pi,0)$ and $1$ on $(0,\pi)$: $\frac4\pi\left(\sin t+\frac{\sin3t}{3}+\cdots\right)$. Differentiating the sawtooth is not allowed: the result does not converge.
:::
