Most systems in nature are nonlinear, and few can be solved by formula. If the rates do not depend on time explicitly, the phase plane still shows their behaviour completely.

::: {#def-autonomous-system .definition name="Autonomous system, critical point"}
A $2\times2$ *autonomous system* is
$$
x'=f(x,y),\qquad y'=g(x,y),
$$
with $f,g$ continuously differentiable and independent of $t$. The *vector field* $(f,g)$ gives the velocity of the solution at each point. A *critical point* is a point where $f=g=0$; it is a constant solution.
:::

::: {#prp-autonomous-trajectories .proposition name="Trajectories do not cross"}
If $(x(t),y(t))$ is a solution, so is $(x(t-c),y(t-c))$ for every constant $c$. Two trajectories that share a point are the same curve; in particular a trajectory never passes through a critical point in finite time.
:::

::: {.proof}
1. Since $f,g$ do not depend on $t$, $\frac{d}{dt}x(t-c)=f\bigl(x(t-c),y(t-c)\bigr)$, and likewise for $y$: the shifted function is a solution.

2. Suppose solutions $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ pass through the same point $P$, at times $t_1$ and $t_2$. The shift $\mathbf b(t-(t_1-t_2))$ is a solution equal to $P$ at time $t_1$.

3. By uniqueness of solutions (the system version of @thm-existence-uniqueness-first-order), it coincides with $\mathbf a$; so the two trajectories are the same curve.

4. A critical point is itself a trajectory, so no other trajectory can reach it at a finite time.
:::

::: {#prp-trajectory-equation .proposition name="The equation of the trajectories"}
Away from points where $f=0$, the trajectories of $x'=f$, $y'=g$ are integral curves of the first-order equation
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{g(x,y)}{f(x,y)} .
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. Along a solution with $x'=f\neq0$, $x$ is locally invertible and the chain rule gives $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y'}{x'}=\frac gf$.
:::

::: {#exm-pendulum .example name="The pendulum"}
A frictionless pendulum at angle $x$ with angular velocity $y$ obeys $x''=-\sin x$, that is
$$
x'=y,\qquad y'=-\sin x .
$$

1. The critical points are $(k\pi,0)$: hanging down for even $k$, balanced upright for odd $k$.
2. By @prp-trajectory-equation, $y\,dy=-\sin x\,dx$, so $E=\tfrac12y^2-\cos x$ is constant on each trajectory: the trajectories are level curves of the energy.
3. For $-1<E<1$ they are closed curves around $(0,0)$ (swinging); for $E>1$ they are wavy lines with $y$ never $0$ (spinning over the top).
:::
