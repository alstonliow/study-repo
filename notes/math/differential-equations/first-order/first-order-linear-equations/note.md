A first-order equation is *linear* when the unknown and its derivative appear only to the first power. Linear equations are the one large class that can always be solved by integration.

::: {#def-first-order-linear .definition name="First-order linear equation"}
A *first-order linear equation* is one that can be written in the *standard form*
$$
y' + p(t)\,y = q(t).
$$
It is *homogeneous* if $q=0$. In models, $q$ is often called the *input* (or forcing) and the solution $y$ the *response*.
:::

::: {#thm-integrating-factor .theorem name="Solution by an integrating factor"}
Let $p$ and $q$ be continuous on an interval $I$ and let $u(t)=e^{\int p(t)\,dt}$ (any antiderivative). Then the solutions of $y'+py=q$ on $I$ are exactly
$$
y(t) = \frac{1}{u(t)}\left(\int u(t)\,q(t)\,dt + C\right),\qquad C\in\mathbb R .
$$

```{=latex}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Integrating-factor method for $y'+p(t)y=q(t)$}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT continuous $p$, $q$; optionally an initial value $y(t_0)=y_0$
\STATE divide by the leading coefficient to reach the standard form $y'+py=q$
\STATE $u\gets e^{\int p\,dt}$ \COMMENT{any antiderivative}
\STATE $Y\gets\int u\,q\,dt+C$
\STATE $y\gets Y/u$
\IF{an initial value is given}
  \STATE solve $y(t_0)=y_0$ for $C$
\ENDIF
\RETURN $y$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

:::

::: {.proof}
1. Since $u'=pu$, the product rule gives $(uy)'=uy'+puy=u\,(y'+py)$.

2. Because $u>0$, the equation $y'+py=q$ holds if and only if $(uy)'=uq$.

3. A function has derivative $uq$ if and only if it equals $\int uq\,dt+C$ for some constant $C$.

4. Dividing by $u$ gives the formula.
:::

::: {#prp-first-order-linear-structure .proposition name="Particular plus homogeneous"}
If $y_p$ is one solution of $y'+py=q$ and $y_h=e^{-\int p\,dt}$, then every solution is
$$
y = y_p + C\,y_h .
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. The difference $y-y_p$ of two solutions satisfies the homogeneous equation $z'+pz=0$.

2. By @thm-integrating-factor with $q=0$, the solutions of that equation are $z=C/u=Cy_h$.
:::

::: {#exm-integrating-factor-power .example name="An integrating factor"}
Solve $ty'+2y=t^2$ for $t>0$.

1. Standard form: $y'+\tfrac{2}{t}y=t$, so $u=e^{2\ln t}=t^2$.
2. Then $(t^2y)'=t^3$, so $t^2y=\tfrac14t^4+C$.
3. Hence $y=\tfrac14t^2+C\,t^{-2}$.
:::

::: {#exm-mixing-tank .example name="A mixing tank"}
A 100-litre tank of pure water receives brine with 2 g/L of salt at 5 L/min, and the well-mixed solution leaves at 5 L/min. The salt $x(t)$ (grams) satisfies
$$
x' = \underbrace{2\cdot 5}_{\text{in}} - \underbrace{\tfrac{x}{100}\cdot 5}_{\text{out}},\qquad x(0)=0,
$$
that is $x'+\tfrac{1}{20}x=10$. With $u=e^{t/20}$ the solution is $x(t)=200\bigl(1-e^{-t/20}\bigr)$, which tends to $200$ g, the salt in a full tank of incoming brine.
:::

::: {#thm-constant-coefficient-first-order .theorem name="Transient and steady state"}
Let $k>0$ and $q$ be continuous. The solution of $y'+ky=q(t)$ with $y(0)=y_0$ is
$$
y(t) = \underbrace{e^{-kt}\int_0^t e^{ks}q(s)\,ds}_{\text{steady-state part}} \;+\; \underbrace{y_0\,e^{-kt}}_{\text{transient}} .
$$
The transient tends to $0$, so for large $t$ all solutions behave alike, whatever $y_0$ is.
:::

::: {.proof}
1. Here $u=e^{kt}$, and integrating $(e^{kt}y)'=e^{kt}q$ from $0$ to $t$ gives $e^{kt}y(t)-y_0=\int_0^te^{ks}q(s)\,ds$.

2. Multiplying by $e^{-kt}$ gives the formula.

3. The second term is $y_0e^{-kt}\to 0$ because $k>0$; two solutions differ only in this term.
:::

::: {#rmk-first-order-linear-equations-1 .remark name="Steady-state solution"}
When $k>0$, any solution may serve as "the" long-term response, and one usually picks the simplest. For a constant input $q=c$ it is the constant $c/k$; for a sinusoidal input it is a sinusoid of the same frequency (@sec-math-differential-equations-complex-and-sinusoids-complex-replacement).
:::
