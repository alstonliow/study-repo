Most first-order equations $y'=f(x,y)$ cannot be solved by a formula. Their solutions can still be *drawn*: the equation tells us the slope of the solution through every point of the plane.

::: {#def-direction-field .definition name="Direction field, integral curve"}
For a first-order equation
$$
y' = f(x,y),
$$
the *direction field* (slope field) attaches to each point $(x,y)$ a short line segment of slope $f(x,y)$. An *integral curve* is a curve that is tangent to the direction field at each of its points; the graphs of solutions are exactly the integral curves that are graphs of functions.
:::

::: {#def-isocline .definition name="Isocline"}
The *isocline* of slope $m$ is the curve
$$
f(x,y) = m .
$$
All segments of the direction field along it have the same slope $m$, so drawing a few isoclines and their slopes is a quick way to sketch the field by hand.
:::

::: {#exm-isoclines-linear .example name="Isoclines of $y'=x-y$"}
For $y'=x-y$ the isocline of slope $m$ is the line $y=x-m$.

1. The isocline of slope $1$ is the line $y=x-1$. Its own slope is $1$, so it is an integral curve: $y=x-1$ is a solution.
2. Above it the slopes are less than $1$ and below it they are greater, so other integral curves bend towards it.
3. Indeed the general solution is $y=x-1+Ce^{-x}$: every solution approaches $y=x-1$ as $x\to\infty$.
:::

The direction field of $y'=x-y$ with the isoclines of slope $0$ and $2$ (dashed). The isocline of slope $1$, $y=x-1$, is itself a solution (thick), and every other solution approaches it.

::: {#thm-existence-uniqueness-first-order .theorem name="Existence and uniqueness"}
If $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous on an open rectangle containing $(x_0,y_0)$, then the initial value problem
$$
y'=f(x,y),\qquad y(x_0)=y_0
$$
has exactly one solution on some open interval containing $x_0$.
:::

::: {#rmk-direction-fields-1 .remark name="Integral curves do not cross"}
Where the theorem applies, two different integral curves can never meet: at a common point both would solve the same initial value problem. This is what makes sketches reliable: an integral curve trapped between two others stays between them.
:::

::: {#exm-nonuniqueness .example name="When uniqueness fails"}
The equation $y'=3y^{2/3}$ with $y(0)=0$ has at least two solutions, $y=0$ and $y=x^3$: indeed $(x^3)'=3x^2=3(x^3)^{2/3}$. The theorem does not apply because $\partial f/\partial y=2y^{-1/3}$ is not continuous at $y=0$.
:::
