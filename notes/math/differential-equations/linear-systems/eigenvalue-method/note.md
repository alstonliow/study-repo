For a constant matrix $A$, the system $\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x$ is solved by exponentials $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ along eigenvectors. This note covers the three cases for a real $2\times2$ matrix.

::: {#def-normal-mode .definition name="Normal mode"}
If $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v\neq\mathbf0$, the solution $\mathbf x=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ of $\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x$ is a *normal mode*: all components change by the same factor $e^{\lambda t}$, and the shape $\mathbf v$ is preserved.
:::

::: {#thm-eigenvalue-method-real .theorem name="Distinct real eigenvalues" related="thm-pure-exponential-solutions"}
If the real $2\times2$ matrix $A$ has distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ with eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$, the general solution of $\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x$ is
$$
\mathbf x=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2 .
$$

```{=latex}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Eigenvalue method for $\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x$, $A$ real $2\times2$}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT real $2\times2$ matrix $A$
\STATE find the roots $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ of $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A$
\IF{$\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$ are real}
  \STATE eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$; \RETURN $c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2$
\ELSIF{$\lambda=a\pm i\omega$ with $\omega\neq0$}
  \STATE eigenvector $\mathbf v$ for $a+i\omega$; \RETURN $c_1\operatorname{Re}(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v)+c_2\operatorname{Im}(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v)$
\ELSIF{$A=\lambda I$}
  \RETURN $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf c$ for any $\mathbf c$
\ELSE
  \STATE eigenvector $\mathbf v$, then solve $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf w=\mathbf v$
  \RETURN $c_1e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v+c_2e^{\lambda t}(t\mathbf v+\mathbf w)$
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

:::

::: {.proof}
1. Each term is a solution, and so is their sum by superposition.

2. Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are independent, so at $t=0$ the vectors $c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2$ fill $\mathbb R^2$: any initial value can be matched.

3. By uniqueness (@thm-existence-uniqueness-linear-systems) every solution is of this form.
:::

::: {#exm-eigenvalue-method-real .example name="A saddle"}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\3&1\end{pmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $4$ and $-2$ with eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(-1,1)$, so
$$
\mathbf x=c_1e^{4t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+c_2e^{-2t}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
:::

::: {#thm-complex-eigenvalue-solutions .theorem name="Complex eigenvalues"}
If the real matrix $A$ has a complex eigenvalue $\lambda=a+i\omega$ ($\omega\neq0$) with eigenvector $\mathbf v$, then the real and imaginary parts of $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ are two independent real solutions of $\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x$.
:::

::: {.proof}
1. $\mathbf z=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ satisfies $\mathbf z'=\lambda\mathbf z=A\mathbf z$.

2. Since $A$ is real, taking real and imaginary parts of $\mathbf z'=A\mathbf z$ gives $(\operatorname{Re}\mathbf z)'=A\operatorname{Re}\mathbf z$ and $(\operatorname{Im}\mathbf z)'=A\operatorname{Im}\mathbf z$.

3. At $t=0$ they equal $\operatorname{Re}\mathbf v$ and $\operatorname{Im}\mathbf v$. These are independent: otherwise $\mathbf v$ would be a complex multiple of a real vector, a real eigenvector, whose eigenvalue would then be real.
:::

::: {#exm-eigenvalue-method-complex .example name="A spiral"}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda=-1+2i$ with $\mathbf v=(i,1)$. Then
$$
e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v=e^{-t}(\cos2t+i\sin2t)\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{so}\quad \mathbf x=c_1e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin2t\\\cos2t\end{pmatrix}+c_2e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos2t\\\sin2t\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Stable spiral for the eigenvalues $-1\pm2i$: solutions turn counterclockwise with angular speed $2$ while shrinking like $e^{-t}$.

:::

::: {#thm-repeated-eigenvalue-solutions .theorem name="A repeated eigenvalue with one eigenvector"}
If the $2\times2$ matrix $A$ has a double eigenvalue $\lambda$ but only one independent eigenvector $\mathbf v$, choose $\mathbf w$ with $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf w=\mathbf v$. Then the general solution is
$$
\mathbf x=c_1e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v+c_2e^{\lambda t}\bigl(t\,\mathbf v+\mathbf w\bigr).
$$
:::

::: {.proof}
1. Such a $\mathbf w$ exists: $N=A-\lambda I$ satisfies $N^2=0$ (Cayley–Hamilton) and $N\neq0$, so the image of $N$ is a line inside its kernel, the line of $\mathbf v$.

2. For $\mathbf x_2=e^{\lambda t}(t\mathbf v+\mathbf w)$: $\mathbf x_2'=e^{\lambda t}\bigl(\lambda t\mathbf v+\lambda\mathbf w+\mathbf v\bigr)$.

3. And $A\mathbf x_2=e^{\lambda t}\bigl(t\lambda\mathbf v+A\mathbf w\bigr)=e^{\lambda t}\bigl(\lambda t\mathbf v+\lambda\mathbf w+\mathbf v\bigr)$, the same.

4. At $t=0$ the two solutions are $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf w$, independent because $N\mathbf w=\mathbf v\neq\mathbf0=N\mathbf v$.
:::

::: {#exm-eigenvalue-method-repeated .example name="A defective matrix"}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda=2$ is a double eigenvalue with only $\mathbf v=(1,1)$. Solving $(A-2I)\mathbf w=\mathbf v$ gives $\mathbf w=(0,1)$, so the second solution is $e^{2t}(t,\,t+1)$.
:::
