::: {#def-measurable-function .definition name="Measurable function"}

Let $(E,\mathcal E)$ and $(G,\mathcal G)$ be measurable spaces. A map $f:E\to G$ is *measurable* if

$$
f^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal E\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal G .
$$

When $G=\mathbb R$ or $[-\infty,\infty]$ the σ-algebra on $G$ is the Borel one unless said otherwise; for $E$ a topological space, a measurable $f:(E,\mathcal B(E))\to\mathbb R$ is *Borel*.

:::

::: {#lem-measurable-generator .lemma name="It suffices to check a generator"}

If $\mathcal G=\sigma(\mathcal Q)$, then $f$ is measurable as soon as $f^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal E$ for all $A\in\mathcal Q$. In particular $f:E\to\mathbb R$ is measurable if and only if $\{f\le a\}\in\mathcal E$ for every $a\in\mathbb R$, and continuous maps between topological spaces are Borel.

:::

::: {.proof}

1. The collection $\{A\subseteq G:\ f^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal E\}$ is a σ-algebra, because preimages commute with complements and unions.

2. It contains $\mathcal Q$, hence $\sigma(\mathcal Q)=\mathcal G$.

3. The half-lines $(-\infty,a]$ generate $\mathcal B$, and the open sets generate the Borel σ-algebra; preimages of open sets under continuous maps are open.

:::

Compositions of measurable maps are measurable. A family of maps $f_i:E\to G_i$ *generates* the σ-algebra $\sigma(f_i:i\in I)=\sigma\big(f_i^{-1}(A):\ i\in I,\ A\in\mathcal G_i\big)$, the smallest one making every $f_i$ measurable. The *product σ-algebra* on $\prod_iG_i$ is the one generated by the coordinate projections; a map $x\mapsto(f_i(x))_i$ into the product is measurable exactly when each $f_i$ is, by @lem-measurable-generator.

::: {#prp-measurable-closure .proposition name="Operations preserving measurability"}

Let $f_1,f_2,\dots:E\to\mathbb R$ be measurable. Then $f_1+f_2$, $f_1f_2$, $f_1\vee f_2=\max(f_1,f_2)$ and $f_1\wedge f_2$ are measurable, and so are the $[-\infty,\infty]$-valued functions

$$
\inf_nf_n,\qquad\sup_nf_n,\qquad\liminf_nf_n,\qquad\limsup_nf_n .
$$

The set where $f_n$ converges in $[-\infty,\infty]$ is measurable.

:::

::: {.proof}

1. $(f_1,f_2):E\to\mathbb R^2$ is measurable, because $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$ is generated by products of intervals, and $(f_1,f_2)^{-1}(I\times J)=f_1^{-1}(I)\cap f_2^{-1}(J)$. Addition, multiplication, max and min are continuous on $\mathbb R^2$, so the compositions are measurable.

2. $\{\sup_nf_n\le a\}=\bigcap_n\{f_n\le a\}$ is measurable for every $a$; the half-lines $[-\infty,a]$ generate $\mathcal B([-\infty,\infty])$. Similarly $\{\inf_nf_n<a\}=\bigcup_n\{f_n<a\}$.

3. $\limsup_nf_n=\inf_m\sup_{n\ge m}f_n$ and $\liminf_nf_n=\sup_m\inf_{n\ge m}f_n$ are measurable by step 2.

4. The convergence set is $\{\liminf_nf_n=\limsup_nf_n\}$, the preimage of the closed diagonal under the measurable map $(\liminf f_n,\limsup f_n)$ into $[-\infty,\infty]^2$.

:::

::: {#prp-dyadic-approximation .proposition name="Approximation by simple functions"}

Let $f:E\to[0,\infty]$ be measurable and put

$$
f_n=\min\big(2^{-n}\lfloor2^nf\rfloor,\ n\big).
$$

Each $f_n$ is a finite linear combination of indicators of measurable sets, $0\le f_n\le f_{n+1}$, and $f_n\uparrow f$ pointwise; if $f$ is bounded, the convergence is uniform.

:::

::: {.proof}

1. $f_n$ takes the finitely many values $k2^{-n}$ ($0\le k\le n2^n$) on the measurable sets $\{k2^{-n}\le f<(k+1)2^{-n}\}$ (and the value $n$ on $\{f\ge n\}$).

2. $2^{-n}\lfloor2^nt\rfloor\le2^{-n-1}\lfloor2^{n+1}t\rfloor$ because $2\lfloor s\rfloor\le\lfloor 2s\rfloor$, so the $f_n$ increase.

3. If $f(x)<\infty$, then for $n\ge f(x)$ we have $0\le f(x)-f_n(x)<2^{-n}$; if $f(x)=\infty$, then $f_n(x)=n\to\infty$. For bounded $f$ the bound $2^{-n}$ is uniform once $n\ge\sup f$.

:::

::: {#thm-monotone-class .theorem name="Monotone class theorem"}

Let $\mathcal A$ be a π-system with $\sigma(\mathcal A)=\mathcal E$, and $V$ a vector space of bounded functions $E\to\mathbb R$ such that

1. $\mathbf 1_E\in V$ and $\mathbf 1_A\in V$ for all $A\in\mathcal A$;
2. if $f_n\in V$, $0\le f_n\uparrow f$ and $f$ is bounded, then $f\in V$.

Then $V$ contains every bounded measurable function.

:::

::: {.proof}

1. Let $\mathcal D=\{A\in\mathcal E:\ \mathbf 1_A\in V\}$. It contains $\mathcal A$ and $E$; if $A\subseteq B$ are in $\mathcal D$, then $\mathbf 1_{B\setminus A}=\mathbf 1_B-\mathbf 1_A\in V$; if $A_n\uparrow A$ with $A_n\in\mathcal D$, then $\mathbf 1_{A_n}\uparrow\mathbf 1_A$, so $A\in\mathcal D$ by hypothesis 2. So $\mathcal D$ is a d-system and by Dynkin's lemma (@lem-dynkin) $\mathcal D=\mathcal E$.

2. A bounded measurable $f\ge0$ is the increasing limit of the simple functions of @prp-dyadic-approximation, which lie in $V$ by step 1 and linearity. So $f\in V$.

3. A bounded measurable $f$ is $f^+-f^-$ with $f^\pm=\pm f\vee0$ bounded, non-negative and measurable, so $f\in V$.

:::

## New measures from measurable maps

::: {#def-image-measure .definition name="Image measure"}

Let $\mu$ be a measure on $(E,\mathcal E)$ and $f:E\to G$ measurable. The *image measure* (push-forward) is

$$
\mu\circ f^{-1}(A)=\mu\big(f^{-1}(A)\big),\qquad A\in\mathcal G .
$$

:::

It is a measure because preimages of disjoint sets are disjoint and preimages commute with unions.

::: {#lem-generalised-inverse .lemma name="Generalised inverse of a monotone function"}

Let $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be non-constant, non-decreasing and right-continuous, and $I=(g(-\infty),g(\infty))$ with $g(\pm\infty)=\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}g(y)$. Then

$$
h(x)=\inf\{y\in\mathbb R:\ x\le g(y)\},\qquad x\in I,
$$

is finite, non-decreasing and left-continuous, and for $x\in I$, $y\in\mathbb R$,

$$
h(x)\le y\iff x\le g(y).
$$

:::

::: {.proof}

1. Fix $x\in I$ and let $J_x=\{y:\ x\le g(y)\}$. Since $x<g(\infty)$, $J_x\neq\emptyset$; since $x>g(-\infty)$, $J_x$ is bounded below. So $h(x)\in\mathbb R$.

2. $J_x$ is an up-set because $g$ is non-decreasing, and it is closed under decreasing limits because $g$ is right-continuous. Hence $J_x=[h(x),\infty)$, which is the stated equivalence.

3. If $x\le x'$ then $J_{x'}\subseteq J_x$, so $h(x)\le h(x')$.

4. If $x_n\uparrow x$ then $J_x=\bigcap_nJ_{x_n}$, i.e. $[h(x),\infty)=\bigcap_n[h(x_n),\infty)$, so $h(x_n)\uparrow h(x)$.

:::

The generalised inverse $h(x)=\inf\{y:\ x\le g(y)\}$. A jump of $g$ becomes a flat piece of $h$ (every $x$ in the gap is sent to the jump point), and a flat piece of $g$ becomes a jump of $h$ (at $x'$, $h$ takes the left end of the flat piece).

::: {#thm-lebesgue-stieltjes .theorem name="Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures"}

Let $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be non-constant, non-decreasing and right-continuous. There is a unique Borel measure $dg$ on $\mathbb R$ with

$$
dg((a,b])=g(b)-g(a)\qquad\text{for all }a<b .
$$

Every non-zero Borel measure on $\mathbb R$ that is finite on bounded sets arises in this way.

:::

::: {.proof}

1. Let $h$ and $I$ be as in @lem-generalised-inverse. $h$ is monotone, hence Borel (each $\{h\le y\}$ is an interval). Let $\lambda_I$ be Lebesgue measure restricted to the Borel subsets of $I$ and put $dg=\lambda_I\circ h^{-1}$.

2. By the lemma, $\{x\in I:\ a<h(x)\le b\}=\{x\in I:\ g(a)<x\le g(b)\}$, whose Lebesgue measure is $g(b)-g(a)$ (the interval $(g(a),g(b)]$ meets $I$ in a set of the same length).

3. Uniqueness: two such measures agree on the π-system of half-open intervals and are finite on each $(n,n+1]$; apply @cor-uniqueness-sigma-finite.

4. Conversely, if $\nu$ is non-zero and finite on bounded sets, put $g(y)=\nu((0,y])$ for $y\ge0$ and $g(y)=-\nu((y,0])$ for $y<0$. Then $g$ is non-decreasing, right-continuous by continuity of $\nu$ from above, non-constant, and $\nu((a,b])=g(b)-g(a)$; so $\nu=dg$ by step 3.

:::
